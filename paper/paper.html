When Retrieval Does Not Help: A Controlled Study of Citation Intent Classification with BM25L Evidence

Chen Boxin
School of Business Administration, Huaqiao University · Quanzhou 362000, China
2416411001@stu.hqu.edu.cn
Abstract. Citation intent classification labels the sentence containing a citation marker as background, method, or result use. We test a natural idea on this task: prepend the input with k similar contexts retrieved by BM25L, so the encoder sees the target sentence together with lexical evidence from neighbours in the training pool. Across BERT, SciBERT and DeBERTa-v3-base on SciCite, under a leakage-controlled protocol, the answer is negative. DeBERTa-v3-base alone reaches 86.89% accuracy and 85.49% macro-F1, and no augmented variant beats it. Three results qualify that headline. First, retrieval-augmented inputs break a standard [CLS] head (accuracy falls to 59.97%), while a gated dual-pooling head that fuses the sentence vector with a pooled evidence vector restores 86.14% / 84.71%, in effect recovering the baseline. The head, not the evidence, is the fragile part. Second, the method is cheap once the engineering is done: a sparse-matrix rewrite of the BM25L scorer cuts a full preprocessing pass over 8,243 contexts from about 8 minutes to about 12 seconds, cached re-builds take under one second, and training throughput is unchanged on a single 6 GB GPU. Third, the one configuration that appears to beat the backbone — a mask that forbids same-label evidence, lifting accuracy to 96.40% — does so because the mask itself leaks: with three classes and k=3 neighbours, the target label is exactly the class absent from the evidence, and the model learns the elimination rule. All code, configurations and run logs are released.

1  Introduction

Citation intent classification (CIC) asks a question bibliometric tools increasingly need answered automatically: given the sentence in which a citation appears, why is the cited work referenced? Cohan et al. [1] framed the task over three intents — background, method and result — and released SciCite, 11k annotated contexts spanning computer science and biomedicine, which remains the standard benchmark.

Pretrained encoders dominate current leaderboards. SciBERT [2] adapted BERT to scientific text; DeBERTa-v3 [3] replaced absolute position embeddings with disentangled attention and transfers well across classification benchmarks. On the retrieval side, RAG [4] and DPR [5] showed that an external corpus, consulted at inference time, lifts performance on open-domain QA — precisely where a single input lacks the information needed to answer.

CIC sits between the two. The input is one sentence, and intent is often recoverable from surface cues: "we apply the method of X" reads as a method citation, "in contrast to Y" as a result comparison. But some contexts are ambiguous in isolation, and similar contexts elsewhere in the corpus carry the same phrases with the label attached. Retrieval augmentation therefore has a plausible mechanism here, and the mechanism is cheap to test: retrieve neighbours with BM25, concatenate, fine-tune.

The mechanism, however, has an obvious failure path: if the retrieved neighbour's label equals the target's, the model can copy rather than learn. Leakage of this kind is easy to introduce and easy to miss. So we ran the study with the safeguards built in — train-only pool, O(1) self-exclusion, and a leak-free mask that forbids same-label evidence — and with three backbones of increasing strength.

The outcome is a clean negative. With safeguards in place, no retrieval-augmented variant beats its plain encoder, and the strongest backbone, DeBERTa-v3-base, stays ahead at 86.89% / 85.49%. The study produced two findings worth reporting in their own right:

  • Diagnostic. Concatenating heterogeneous evidence silently breaks a standard [CLS] head (59.97% accuracy), and a gated dual-pooling head fixes this, restoring the augmented model to within 0.8 points of its backbone. If one runs retrieval augmentation and sees it fail — or sees it "work" after removing a leakage control — the pooling head and the retrieval pool are the first places to look.
  • Practical. The full pipeline, including a fresh preprocessing pass over all 8,243 training contexts, runs in seconds on the same 6 GB GPU that trains the models, so reruns and ablations cost almost nothing once the caching layer is in place.

2  Related Work

Teufel et al. [11] classified citation function with argumentative zones and hand-crafted features. Jurgens et al. [10] built ACL-ARC, six classes over computational linguistics papers. SciCite [1] scaled the annotation effort to 11k contexts and added structural scaffolds on top of ELMo [12]. BERT [6] set the template; SciBERT [2] pre-trained from scratch on Semantic Scholar corpora; DeBERTa-v3 [3] combined disentangled attention with ELECTRA-style pre-training [13]. REALM [7], RAG [4] and DPR [5] retrieve-then-read for knowledge-intensive generation; outside QA, sparse retrievers still find use because their evidence is cheap and inspectable — BM25 [8] and its length-normalised variant BM25L [9] remain strong baselines.

3  Method

3.1  Task and baselines

p(y | s) = softmax( W·h + b ),   h = CLS( E( tokenise(s) ) )

Three encoders are benchmarked — BERT-base, SciBERT, DeBERTa-v3-base — sharing tokeniser settings, training schedule and head, so that encoder is the only variable.

3.2  Retrieval-augmented inputs

Ēk(s) = top-kc∈𝒞 [ BM25L(s, c) ],    s̃ = e1 ⊕ … ⊕ ek ⊕ s

BM25L scores s against every c in the pool after lowercase, punctuation stripping and whitespace folding. δ = 0.5 lifts the lower bound of term-frequency normalisation, which matters for citation snippets whose lengths range from a handful of words to sixty.

3.3  Leakage control

  1. Train-only pool — nothing is ever retrieved from the evaluation splits.
  2. O(1) self-exclusion — the query is excluded from its own results via a text-to-index map.
  3. Leak-free mask — for one ablation, evidence whose label equals the target's is forbidden, isolating the label-copying failure path. It turned out to isolate an elimination leak instead (§4.3).

3.4  Gated dual-pooling head

h = g ⊙ hcls + (1−g) ⊙ hev,   g = σ( Wg[hcls; hev] + bg )

An augmented input mixes evidence segments and the target sentence; the standard head reads only the pooled [CLS] position and cannot tell the two apart. The gate mixes a sentence-level vector with an evidence-pooled vector before the linear head. Extra parameters: one linear layer.

3.5  Fast, cacheable preprocessing

Vectorised BM25L. The reference implementation (rank_bm25) scores one query term by looping over every corpus document in Python. We store term frequencies in a sparse CSC matrix and score each query term by touching only its nonzeros. Over all 8,243 training queries the two implementations agree to float64 rounding (max difference 5.5e-12), every query returns the same top-3, and the retrieved evidence is byte-identical. The scoring pass drops from 7.0 minutes to 5.5 seconds; the full augmented build from 8.3 minutes to 11.6 seconds.

Disk cache. Augmented datasets are pickled once per retrieval configuration (keyed by k, leak-free flag and index version). Any rerun that reuses the configuration loads from disk in about half a second, byte-identical to the fresh pass.

4  Experiments

4.1  Data and protocol

SciCite [1]: 11,020 contexts (train 8,243 / dev 916 / test 1,861), background ~58%, method ~29%, result ~13%. Checkpoints by dev macro-F1; 3 epochs, AdamW, LR 2e-5, effective batch 32, max length 256, seed 42. Hardware: a single RTX 3060 (6 GB). bf16 and gradient checkpointing are on; the backbone loads in fp32 with contiguous parameters — mixed fp16/fp32 weights, in our runs, converge to majority-class prediction while the training loss keeps decreasing, so we fix dtype at load time rather than mid-training.

Retrieval with a bare CLS head fails outright — 59.97% accuracy is barely above the majority-class rate — and training completes without complaint, which makes the failure easy to miss. The dual head repairs this: both dual-pooling runs land within 0.8 points of the DeBERTa baseline (shown separately to indicate run-to-run variance). Under the leakage controls of §3.3, we found no configuration with label-blind inputs in which retrieval improved either metric over the corresponding plain encoder; the one label-conditioned exception is treated in §4.3 as a leak in its own right.

The dual-pooling head accounts for 26 points of accuracy against the CLS baseline on identical data. The dtype fix accounts for a further silent failure: with half-precision backbone weights and an fp32 head, the same run collapses toward the majority class while its loss curve looks healthy. Both failures are invisible from the loss alone, and both are cheap to prevent once known.

The leak-free row posts the best numbers in the paper and is the least trustworthy of them. The mask removes every candidate whose label equals the target's, so the k=3 neighbours that survive carry only the other two classes; the target label is then the class absent from the input, and the encoder learns the elimination rule (96.40% test accuracy, 97.88% train accuracy — the shortcut transfers perfectly, which is what a shortcut looks like). The lesson is not that retrieval finally helped. It is that a leakage control conditioned on the label changes the input distribution in label-correlated ways: anything conditioned on y at input-construction time is a channel, whether it adds evidence or removes it. Our default protocol retrieves label-blind, so the honest comparison is the rows above.

Scoring agrees to float64 rounding, every query returns the same top-3, and the retrieved evidence is byte-identical for all 8,243 queries. The training comparison needs one caveat to read fairly: the plain baseline pads every context to 256 tokens although the median context is 48 tokens long, so both configurations push about 2.1M tokens per epoch through the encoder; on that shared budget the two models train at the same throughput and evaluate within 2.5% of each other. For practical purposes the augmentation is free at training time; it costs 12 seconds before training starts, once per configuration. The full grid behind this paper — three baselines, three augmented runs, the ablation diagnostics — ran on one 6 GB GPU in a working session and can be reproduced from a cold start in minutes rather than hours.

4.5  Why does retrieval not help here?

The evidence BM25L returns is lexical. On citation contexts, lexical similarity tracks label-specific phrasing, which the encoder already reads off the target sentence directly. The retrieved neighbours add, at best, a redundant restatement of what the backbone can extract in one pass; at worst, confusion — which is exactly what the CLS-head collapse shows. The dual head recovers the baseline rather than exceeding it because the gate can learn to down-weight the evidence entirely; nothing in the augmented input contains information the plain input lacks.

This is a claim about CIC under a strong in-domain encoder, not about retrieval augmentation in general. QA needs the corpus because the question does not contain the answer; here the sentence, for most instances, does contain the label signal. Where that stops holding — multi-intent taxonomies, cross-domain transfer, contexts whose meaning depends on the cited abstract — the balance may shift. Our protocol, with its leakage controls and head ablation, is the reusable part: it is how one finds out.

5  Conclusion

We set out to test whether BM25L retrieval augmentation improves citation intent classification under leakage controls, with strong pretrained backbones, on public data. It does not: DeBERTa-v3-base at 86.89% / 85.49% outperforms every augmented variant we measured, and the single variant that outscores it does so through an elimination leak introduced by its own label-conditioned mask. The study's value lies in what it isolates. A standard CLS head silently breaks on augmented inputs, and a gated dual-pooling head recovers them, a 26-point swing anyone running this recipe should know about before drawing conclusions. And the cost of finding such things out is now small: vectorised scoring plus caching brings a full preprocessing pass under 12 seconds and a cached rebuild under half a second, with training throughput unchanged on 6 GB of VRAM. Dense retrievers, cited-abstract fusion, and harder taxonomies such as MultiCite are the natural next tests for the protocol.
